IMF · Programme Analysis

Pakistan and the IMF:
25 Arrangements and Counting

Pakistan is the world's most frequent IMF-borrower, having engaged with the multilateral lender a record 25 times. It is among the four countries classified by the IMF as "prolonged users" of its resources. Pakistan first turned to the IMF in 1958, just over a decade after independence. At present, Pakistan has a $7 billion arrangement with the Fund under the Extended Fund Facility (EFF), along with additional support through the Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF).

Reforms Undertaken

Reforms already undertaken as part of the Extended Fund Facility (EFF) programme.
  • Agricultural Income Tax reforms.
  • Push captive gas users on to the electricity grid.
  • Parliamentary approval of 2026 budget in line with IMF's agreement.

Upcoming Reforms

Next steps mandated under the programme for the release of funds.
  • Privatize the electricity distribution companies of Faisalabad, Islamabad, and Gujranwala (FESCO, IESCO, GEPCO).
  • Liberalization of the sugar market.
  • Reduce the state's footprint in the economy.

Pakistan & the IMF: A Timeline

Six decades of recurring engagement, punctuated by political instability, climate disasters, and geopolitical shocks.

Early Agreements
Pakistan's early agreements with the IMF were mainly Stand-By Arrangements (SBAs) — short-term loans that provide support to meet urgent financing needs.

A period marked by political instability. Pakistan entered into nine different agreements with the IMF in this window alone.

Pakistan entered into an Extended Fund Facility (EFF) — a longer-term programme meant to fix deep and persistent imbalances in the economy.

Political instability — compounded by the devastating 2022 floods — caused the EFF programme to go off track.

Back to the Fund
With reserves running dangerously low, Pakistan once again turned to the IMF, signing another Stand-By Arrangement.

Pakistan entered into another Extended Fund Facility amid continued fiscal and external pressures.

World Bank · Portfolio Review

The World Bank in Pakistan: Social Protection, Energy Reform, and Evolving Conditionalities

The World Bank Group maintains one of its largest country programmes globally in Pakistan. With a cumulative lending portfolio approaching $44 billion and an active pipeline exceeding $12 billion, the Bank's footprint spans virtually every major sector of the Pakistani economy — from social protection and education to energy, transport, and climate resilience.

BISP and the Social Protection Agenda. Perhaps the Bank's most visible intervention is its co-financing and technical support for the Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP), now rebranded under the Kafaalat framework. The programme reaches approximately 9 million beneficiary families and is increasingly central to Pakistan's strategy for cushioning the social costs of IMF-mandated fiscal adjustment.

Energy Sector Lending and Circular Debt. Energy sector reform accounts for a significant portion of the Bank's active portfolio in Pakistan. The circular debt crisis — a structural accumulation of payment arrears across the power sector — has become a major focus of World Bank policy dialogue and conditioned lending.

Climate Finance and IDA21. Following the catastrophic 2022 floods, the Bank deployed emergency financing and has since committed to scaling climate-related lending under the new IDA21 replenishment cycle. Pakistan is designated as a climate-vulnerable country eligible for enhanced concessional terms.

Asian Development Bank · Programme Analysis

ADB and Pakistan: Infrastructure Investment, Energy Reform, and Multilateral Coordination

The Asian Development Bank is Pakistan's single largest multilateral development partner by annual approved financing. In 2025, the ADB committed approximately $3.67 billion to Pakistan — a 22% increase from the prior year — funding fiscal reform, infrastructure, women's economic empowerment, mineral sector development, and education programmes.

Infrastructure and Transport Corridors. ADB's lending to Pakistan's transport sector has historically focused on national highway upgrades, regional connectivity, and urban mass transit, including co-financing of the Lahore Orange Line Metro project.

Energy Sector: Overlap with IMF Conditionalities. ADB has financed generation capacity, grid upgrades, and distribution efficiency programmes that are conceptually aligned with the IMF's circular debt resolution benchmarks and the World Bank's distribution company reform agenda.

Climate Finance and Disaster Resilience. Following the 2022 floods, the ADB has increased its commitments to climate adaptation and disaster risk reduction in Pakistan under its Climate Change Action Plan.
