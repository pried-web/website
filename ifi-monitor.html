IMF · Programme Analysis

Pakistan and the IMF:
25 Arrangements and Counting

The International Monetary Fund (IMF, or the Fund) is the lender of last resort for countries facing, or at risk of, balance-of-payments crises. Its lending is conditional: countries must implement agreed policy measures and reforms before funds are released. These conditionalities are one of the principal ways through which the Fund influences economic policy in borrowing countries, with consequences for their citizens, economies, public finances and environment. This makes the Fund's programmes important to scrutinize — not only to track what Pakistan has committed to, but also to examine how these commitments are implemented and what they mean for the country.

Pakistan is the world's most frequent IMF borrower, having entered into 25 arrangements with the Fund. It is among the four countries classified by the IMF as "prolonged users" of its resources. Pakistan first turned to the IMF in 1958, just over a decade after independence. At present, Pakistan has a $7 billion arrangement with the Fund under the Extended Fund Facility (EFF), along with additional support through the climate-focused Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF).

Reforms Undertaken

Reforms already undertaken as part of the Extended Fund Facility (EFF) programme.
  • Agricultural Income Tax reforms.
  • Push captive gas users on to the electricity grid.
  • Parliamentary approval of 2026 budget in line with IMF's agreement.

Upcoming Reforms

Next steps mandated under the programme for the release of funds.
  • Privatize the electricity distribution companies of Faisalabad, Islamabad, and Gujranwala (FESCO, IESCO, GEPCO).
  • Liberalization of the sugar market.
  • Reduce the state's footprint in the economy.

Pakistan & the IMF: A Timeline

Six decades of recurring engagement, punctuated by political instability, climate disasters, and geopolitical shocks.

1958
Early Agreements
Pakistan's early agreements with the IMF were mainly Stand-By Arrangements (SBAs) — short-term loans that provide support to meet urgent financing needs.
1988 – 2000
Nine Arrangements in Twelve Years
A period marked by political instability. Pakistan entered into nine different agreements with the IMF in this window alone.
2019
A Longer-Term Fix
Pakistan entered into an Extended Fund Facility (EFF) — a longer-term programme meant to fix deep and persistent imbalances in the economy.
2022 🌊
Off Track
Political instability — compounded by the devastating 2022 floods — caused the EFF programme to go off track.
2023
Back to the Fund
With reserves running dangerously low, Pakistan once again turned to the IMF, signing another Stand-By Arrangement.
2024
A New EFF
Pakistan entered into another Extended Fund Facility amid continued fiscal and external pressures.
2025
Climate Finance Added
Pakistan added the Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF) — tying continued Fund support to climate-related reform milestones.

World Bank · Portfolio Review

The World Bank in Pakistan

Pakistan's relationship with the World Bank is one of the longest and most extensive in the developing world. Since joining the institution in 1950, Pakistan has received close to $50 billion in assistance. Today, it has one of the largest active portfolios of the World Bank, with over 100 ongoing projects. These cover areas like energy, water, education, and climate resilience. In 2025, the World Bank and the Government of Pakistan launched a new decade-long Country Partnership Framework (CPF), worth about $20 billion, to support economic stabilization and inclusive growth.

History of the World Bank

The World Bank was established in 1944 as the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD), alongside the International Monetary Fund (IMF) at Bretton Woods. Initially, its purpose was to rebuild war-torn economies after the Second World War. Over time, however, its focus shifted toward long-term development financing in low- and middle-income countries, guided by its twin goals of ending extreme poverty and promoting shared prosperity. The Bank is now part of a group of five institutions called the World Bank Group (WBG). For developing countries like Pakistan, it mainly works through the IBRD and the International Development Association (IDA), which gives concessional loans and grants.

Early Engagements and Landmark Projects

Pakistan's engagement with the Bank dates back to the early years of its independence.

1960
The Indus Waters Treaty
In the 1950s, the World Bank played a crucial mediating role in resolving the Indus Waters dispute between Pakistan and India. This mediation culminated in the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, which remains a cornerstone of water management and regional stability to this day.
1970s
The Tarbela Dam
The Bank helped finance the Tarbela Dam, one of Pakistan's most ambitious infrastructure projects, on the Indus River. While it provided much-needed hydropower and irrigation benefits, it also displaced thousands of people and submerged dozens of villages, raising concerns about the Bank's resettlement and environmental safeguards.
1988
Private Sector Energy Development Project
By the late 1980s, Pakistan faced chronic power shortages that constrained economic growth. The World Bank responded by launching the Private Sector Energy Development Project (PSEDP), aimed at attracting private investment to expand power generation capacity.
1994
Policy Framework for Private Power
PSEDP laid the groundwork for Pakistan's 1994 Policy Framework and Package of Incentives for Private Sector Power Generation, which offered generous fiscal and tariff guarantees to Independent Power Producers (IPPs). The policy led to the establishment of the country's first major private power plant, HUBCO, and marked the beginning of large-scale private participation in Pakistan's energy sector.

The Costs of Liberalization

While these reforms were meant to mobilize private capital and end energy shortages, they also introduced structural imbalances that continue to burden Pakistan's economy. By the late 1990s, the share of hydropower in electricity generation had fallen from 47% to 29%, while reliance on imported fossil fuels rose sharply. Between 1996 and 2001, electricity tariffs increased by about 60%, and the cost of purchasing power from IPPs came to account for nearly half of WAPDA's operating expenses.

The heavy contractual obligations under the Bank-backed policy soon led to disputes. In 1998, the government issued multiple Notices of Intent to Terminate against IPPs on grounds of corruption and technical irregularities, while private producers accused the state of violating contracts. Both sides eventually turned to the World Bank for arbitration, highlighting the tensions between its role as a policy advisor and that of a neutral mediator.

The Energy Paradox

Ironically, while the World Bank's 1990s policy advice helped entrench a carbon-intensive and expensive fuel mix, it now seeks to reverse those very outcomes. Under the new Program for Affordable and Clean Energy (PACE), valued at $40 billion, the Bank aims to reduce generation costs and decarbonize Pakistan's energy sector by promoting renewable energy and efficiency reforms. However, this effort underscores a paradox: the same institution that once encouraged fossil fuel–based private power expansion must now help the country pivot toward cleaner alternatives.

Conclusion

The World Bank has played a defining role in shaping Pakistan's development trajectory, from mediating the Indus Waters Treaty and financing dams to restructuring the power sector and advising on private participation. While its projects have delivered institutional support, they have also produced social, environmental, and fiscal trade-offs. The Bank's challenge now lies in reconciling its historical emphasis on liberalization with the urgent need for inclusive and sustainable growth. To move beyond past missteps, its future engagement must ensure that development financing strengthens not only economic stability but also social equity, environmental resilience, and energy justice in Pakistan.

Asian Development Bank · Programme Analysis

The Asian Development Bank & Pakistan

Pakistan has been a member of the Asian Development Bank (ADB) since 1966 and is one of the institution's largest recipients of development financing. As of end-2025, ADB had committed $45.8 billion through 789 public-sector loans, grants, guarantees and technical assistance to Pakistan. Its support spans infrastructure, energy, agriculture, social protection, public finance and private-sector development. ADB recently launched a new Country Partnership Strategy (CPS) for Pakistan covering 2026–2030.
